Law School AI Policies Are Becoming Shadow Curricula

Instructors may think they are merely drafting use policies, but students hear something broader: what counts as authorship, when delegation becomes suspect, and whether legal craft is defined by process, product, or disclosure.

Issue 001 / AI accountability

Article 7 min read Education and Practice

Every AI policy in a classroom teaches more than it says. A blanket ban implies that legitimacy depends on friction and solitude. A permissive rule with disclosure requirements suggests that judgment lies in editorial control rather than initial generation. A vague rule tells students that ambiguity is itself a feature of professional life.

That is why these policies matter beyond plagiarism concerns. Law schools are not just regulating a tool. They are implicitly training future lawyers how to think about delegation, verification, and responsibility in environments where machine assistance will be routine.

The policy memo becomes part of the pedagogy, whether or not the syllabus says so.

From integrity to workflow

Many institutional policies still frame AI almost exclusively as an academic integrity issue. That is too narrow. In practice, the harder questions are workflow questions. When is machine drafting acceptable? What level of checking is expected? What forms of reliance undermine independent judgment?

Students need answers that map onto real professional settings. A future-facing policy would treat AI use less like a contraband problem and more like a supervised practice problem.

What students actually learn

Students notice which uses are condemned, which are quietly tolerated, and which are impossible to distinguish from ordinary research support. Those norms become habits. Over time, the hidden lesson may matter more than the formal rule itself.
